# 07 — Survivor experience and barriers to speaking up

Compiled 2026-10-07 for Matra, a NJ-focused, zero-knowledge intake tool for sexual-assault survivors.
All downloads come from publishers, government sites, author or institutional sites, or PubMed Central. Paywalled items are listed as citations only in `paywalled_citations_and_pornography_evidence.md`.

---

## Founder's themes vs. the evidence

| Theme (founder's notes) | What the evidence actually says | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| **Stigma / why survivors don't speak up** | Most rapes and sexual assaults are never reported to police: 65% unreported in NCVS 2006–2010, and 80% of college-student victimizations (BJS). The leading reasons are "personal matter," fear of reprisal, a belief that police won't help, not wanting others to know, and not labeling the event as rape (Cohn et al. 2013). Negative reactions to a first disclosure (blame, disbelief, insensitivity) push survivors to stop disclosing entirely (Ahrens 2006) and predict worse PTSD and depression (Hakimi et al. 2018). | **High.** Consistent across national surveys and qualitative work. |
| **Perpetrators are usually known to the victim** | NISVS 2016/17: among female rape victims, 56% were raped by an acquaintance, 39% by an intimate partner, 16% by a family member, and **only 12% by a stranger**. BJS NCVS: the offender was known in about 80% of rape and sexual assault victimizations against women aged 18–24. Assaults by someone the victim knows *well* are less likely to be reported (62% vs 51% unreported for violent crime overall). | **High.** Large probability samples; categories overlap because some victims had several perpetrators. |
| **"It's complicated"** | Supported. Many survivors don't initially call what happened rape, especially when it doesn't match the "stranger with a weapon" script (Cohn et al. "nonacknowledgment" factor; Ahrens). Many also stay socially or economically tied to the perpetrator (partner, family, coworker). Institutions can add harm through *institutional betrayal* (Smith & Freyd 2014). | **High** for the phenomenon; measurement varies. |
| **Survivors get defamed / framed as the problem (Depp v. Heard)** | DARVO (Deny, Attack, Reverse Victim and Offender) is a documented perpetrator tactic. In experiments it makes observers rate the victim as *less believable and more responsible* (Harsey & Freyd 2020, n = 316 and 360), and education about DARVO partly counteracts this. Retaliatory defamation suits and threats against people who make accusations are well described in legal commentary. For **Depp v. Heard specifically**, the evidence of a broad chilling effect is mostly **anecdotal** (advocates' reports, AP 2022). Experts disagree: Debra Katz called it a celebrity outlier. The jury found Heard's statements false and made with actual malice, while a 2020 UK court found the opposite on similar allegations. The defensible claim is that such suits *can* chill speech and *are* sometimes used tactically, not that this case proves survivors are typically lying or typically silenced. NJ's **UPEPA (2023)** now lets defendants seek early dismissal of SLAPP suits over speech on matters of public concern, with mandatory fee-shifting when the defendant prevails. | DARVO effect: **moderate–high** (experimental, student samples). Chilling effect of Depp v. Heard: **low–moderate** (anecdotal). UPEPA text: **certain**; how it applies to private accusations: **unsettled**. |
| **Disparities (women of color, poorer women)** | NISVS lifetime rape prevalence: **multiracial women 48.0%, AI/AN women 43.7%**, Black 29.0%, White 28.1%, Hispanic 19.7%, Asian/PI 17.2%. Past-12-month rape is higher for Black women (4.0%) than White women (2.0%). Black and Latina survivors face added disclosure barriers: racial loyalty, stereotypes, and distrust of police. They are less likely to disclose. Black women showed larger mental-health harm from *low-to-moderate* negative reactions (Hakimi et al. 2018). Poverty and sexual violence reinforce each other: in a PA survey of rape crisis centers, 70–90% of survivors served were on public assistance or struggling to meet basic needs (PCAR/NSVRC 2007). | **High** for prevalence differences. **Moderate** for mechanisms (mostly qualitative and convenience samples). Note: the Black/White prevalence gap in *lifetime* rape is small in NISVS; the biggest gaps involve AI/AN and multiracial women. |
| **Culture / society / prevention (family, education)** | CDC STOP SV (2016) gives 5 evidence-based strategies: promote protective social norms, teach skills, empower girls and women (including economically), create protective environments, and support survivors. Rigorously evaluated examples: Safe Dates (56–92% less dating-violence victimization and perpetration vs controls, sustained 4 years), Green Dot (11% lower SV perpetration on the intervention campus), Coaching Boys into Men, Strong African American Families (parenting). Rape myth acceptance (IRMA scale) is a measurable cultural driver of blame. | **Moderate–high.** Several RCTs, but effects vary by setting. |
| **Pornography as a societal factor (Fight the New Drug)** | **Contested.** Wright et al. 2016 (22 studies, 7 countries) find a significant correlation with actual sexual aggression, stronger for verbal aggression and possibly amplified by violent content. Ferguson & Hartley 2022 find *no* association for nonviolent porn, a *weak* one for violent porn, weak longitudinal evidence, and a strong role for study quality and researcher bias. Both agree violent content is where any signal is. Causality isn't established; selection effects are plausible. FTND is an advocacy group. | **Low–moderate** for any causal claim. **Moderate** for correlation with *violent* content. |

**Overall for Matra:** the strongest evidence is about *barriers to disclosure*: known perpetrators, non-acknowledgment, fear of not being believed, negative first reactions, and institutional betrayal. That is exactly the problem a private, survivor-controlled documentation tool addresses. Lead with this in pitches and grants. Treat pornography as a contested societal question that doesn't need to be part of the product narrative.

## Item-by-item

### 1. Why survivors don't report

**`bjs_rape_sexual_assault_college_age_females_1995_2013.pdf`**
Sinozich, S., & Langton, L. (2014). *Rape and Sexual Assault Victimization Among College-Age Females, 1995–2013* (NCJ 248471). Bureau of Justice Statistics. https://bjs.ojp.gov/content/pub/pdf/rsavcaf9513.pdf
Uses NCVS data on women aged 18–24. **80%** of student and **67%** of nonstudent victimizations went unreported to police. The offender was known to the victim in **about 80%** of cases. Among those who didn't report, 26%/23% (students/nonstudents) called it a "personal matter," **20%** feared reprisal, and 12%/5% said it was "not important enough." Fewer than 1 in 5 received help from a victim services agency.
*Implication for Matra:* a known-offender, non-police pathway (private documentation first, decide later) fits how most survivors actually behave.

**`bjs_victimizations_not_reported_2006_2010.pdf`**
Langton, L., Berzofsky, M., Krebs, C., & Smiley-McDonald, H. (2012). *Victimizations Not Reported to the Police, 2006–2010* (NCJ 238536). BJS. https://bjs.ojp.gov/content/pub/pdf/vnrp0610.pdf
**65%** of rape and sexual assault victimizations went unreported, the second-highest share of any crime type. **28%** of unreported rapes and sexual assaults went unreported because of fear of reprisal or of getting the offender in trouble, the highest of any crime. The share of victims who didn't report because "police would not or could not help" doubled from 10% (1994) to 20% (2010).
*Implication for Matra:* fear of reprisal is a top barrier, so zero-knowledge storage and no automatic reporting are core safety features, not extras.

**`ahrens_2006_being_silenced.md`**
Ahrens, C. E. (2006). Being silenced: The impact of negative social reactions on the disclosure of rape. *American Journal of Community Psychology, 38*(3–4), 263–274. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10464-006-9069-9 (PMC1705531: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1705531/)
Qualitative study of 8 survivors who disclosed and then stopped for long periods. The study found three routes to silence: (1) negative reactions from professionals (police, medical staff, pastors who laughed, blamed or were cold) made survivors doubt disclosure would help; (2) negative reactions from family and friends reinforced self-blame; (3) responders who failed to confirm "this was rape" left survivors unsure whether it counted.
*Implication for Matra:* the first "listener" matters. Intake copy should be validating and non-judgmental, and should never make the survivor prove the event "qualifies."

**`ullman_et_al_2020_reasons_for_nondisclosure_diverse_sample.md`**
Ullman, S. E., O'Callaghan, E., Shepp, V., & Harris, C. (2020). Reasons for and experiences of sexual assault nondisclosure in a diverse community sample. *Journal of Family Violence, 35*(8), 839–851. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10896-020-00141-9 (PMC7967004)
Interviews with 42 ethnically diverse Chicago-area women. Reasons for not telling or delaying: fear of negative reactions, no perceived support, not wanting to burden others, family and cultural norms, and fear of gendered reactions (men responding with violence, women being overwhelmed). The review notes 19–48% never disclose to informal supporters, and that younger, Black and Latina survivors are less likely to disclose.
*Implication for Matra:* support selective disclosure. Let survivors decide who sees what and when, including family-safe modes.

*(Paywalled, see the citations file: Cohn et al. 2013, three non-reporting factors; Campbell 2008 American Psychologist on "second rape"; Campbell & Raja 1999.)*

### 2. Institutional betrayal

**`smith_freyd_2014_institutional_betrayal.pdf`**
Smith, C. P., & Freyd, J. J. (2014). Institutional betrayal. *American Psychologist, 69*(6), 575–587. https://doi.org/10.1037/a0037564 (author copy: https://dynamic.uoregon.edu/jjf/articles/sf2014.pdf)
Defines institutional betrayal as harm done by trusted institutions (schools, churches, the military) when they fail to prevent abuse or respond supportively to it, whether through omission or commission. The authors earlier found that institutional betrayal interacts with sexual assault to worsen anxiety, dissociation, sexual dysfunction and other trauma outcomes. Proposes the Institutional Betrayal Questionnaire and "institutional courage" as the remedy.
*Implication for Matra:* Matra is itself an institution. Design and policies (data handling, attorney vetting, responsiveness) should be audited against IBQ-style failure modes.

### 3. DARVO

**`harsey_freyd_2020_darvo.pdf`**
Harsey, S., & Freyd, J. J. (2020). Deny, Attack, and Reverse Victim and Offender (DARVO): What is the influence on perceived perpetrator and victim credibility? *Journal of Aggression, Maltreatment & Trauma, 29*(8), 897–916. https://doi.org/10.1080/10926771.2020.1774695 (author copy: https://dynamic.uoregon.edu/jjf/articles/hf2020.pdf)
Two vignette experiments with university students (n = 316, n = 360). Exposure to a perpetrator's DARVO response made participants see the victim as **less believable, more responsible and more abusive**, and the perpetrator as less abusive and less responsible. A short DARVO education condition **reduced** these effects.
*Implication for Matra:* contemporaneous, timestamped documentation helps survivors and attorneys counter later "she's the real abuser" narratives. Attorney-facing materials could include DARVO education.

**`aals_darvo_handout.pdf`** (supplementary)
Richards, T. N. (2019). *Overview of DARVO* (handout, AALS Annual Meeting, Jan. 3, 2019). https://am.aals.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/4/2019/01/AM19DARVOHandout.pdf
A one-page summary of individual and institutional DARVO for a legal-academic audience.
*Implication for Matra:* a ready primer to share with partner attorneys.

### 4. Defamation, silencing, anti-SLAPP, Depp v. Heard

**`harvard_crcl_how_defamation_is_used_to_silence_survivors.md`**
Medina, A. *How defamation is used to silence survivors.* Harvard Civil Rights–Civil Liberties Law Review (online/blog). https://journals.law.harvard.edu/crcl/how-defamation-is-used-to-silence-survivors/
A student-authored advocacy essay arguing that defamation suits and threats (cease-and-desist letters) are increasingly used against survivors who speak publicly. It argues the risk falls hardest on low-wage workers and erodes informal "whisper network" accountability, and it calls for reform. This is opinion, not empirical research.
*Implication for Matra:* survivors need clear guidance that *public* accusations carry legal risk, while private documentation and attorney consultation do not carry the same risk.

**`ap_2022_depp_heard_verdict_metoo.md`**
Dale, M., & Noveck, J. (2022, June). *Depp-Heard trial: Advocates fear chilling effect on accusers.* Associated Press, as syndicated by the Hawaii Tribune-Herald. https://www.hawaiitribune-herald.com/?p=282270 (partial text)
The jury awarded Depp $10M (Heard $2M on her counterclaim). A UK judge in 2020 had found Depp assaulted Heard on a dozen occasions. A Georgia DV clinic reported a caller afraid of being "seen to be a liar like Amber Heard." On TikTok, #JusticeForJohnnyDepp had about 20B views vs about 78M for #JusticeForAmberHeard. #MeToo attorney Debra Katz called the case a celebrity outlier and pointed to Weinstein's failed appeal the same week.
*Implication for Matra:* the trial is a real perception problem for survivors' willingness to come forward. Cite it as a fear driver, not as evidence of a legal trend.

**`volokh_2022_libel_and_chilling_effects.md`**
Volokh, E. (2022, June 1). *Johnny Depp, Amber Heard, libel, and chilling effects.* The Volokh Conspiracy (Reason). https://reason.com/volokh/2022/06/01/libel-and-chilling-effects/
A First Amendment scholar's counterpoint. He agrees libel liability chills even *true* #MeToo allegations, and also chills true "they lied" counter-allegations. He explains that US law (NYT v. Sullivan) deliberately accepts liability for knowing falsehoods, and the jury found actual malice by clear and convincing evidence.
*Implication for Matra:* gives a balanced framing. The legal risk comes from *public* statements a jury might find knowingly false, which is why attorney-guided disclosure matters.

**`nj_upepa_2023_PL2023c155_legislative_history.pdf`**
NJ State Law Library. Legislative history for P.L.2023, c.155 (S2802), the *Uniform Public Expression Protection Act*, N.J.S.A. 2A:53A-49 to -61, signed 9/7/2023 and effective about 10/7/2023. Includes bill text and statements. https://repo.njstatelib.org//bitstreams/ffc0bb62-3601-4890-94fa-266768f59ea9/download
The Act covers suits based on communications in legislative, executive or judicial proceedings, and on speech, press, assembly, petition or association on a **matter of public concern**. The defendant may seek expedited dismissal by order to show cause within 60 days of service. Proceedings, including discovery, are presumptively stayed. The court **shall** award costs and reasonable attorney's fees to a prevailing moving party. It does not apply to claims against government units.
*Implication for Matra:* NJ survivors sued over public statements or court filings have a strong early-dismissal and fee-shifting tool. Whether a private accusation (e.g., to an employer or friends) is a "matter of public concern" is unsettled, so flag this for partner attorneys and don't promise protection.

### 5. Disparities / intersectionality

**`cdc_nisvs_2016_2017_report_on_sexual_violence.pdf`**
Basile, K. C., Smith, S. G., Kresnow, M., Khatiwada, S., & Leemis, R. W. (2022). *The National Intimate Partner and Sexual Violence Survey: 2016/2017 Report on Sexual Violence.* CDC. https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/124625 (fetched via Internet Archive copy of the cdc.gov PDF because CDC blocked automated download)
1 in 4 women (26.8%) and 1 in 26 men (3.8%) report completed or attempted rape. 1 in 9 men were made to penetrate. Lifetime rape among women: multiracial 48.0%, AI/AN 43.7%, Black 29.0%, White 28.1%, Hispanic 19.7%, Asian/PI 17.2%. Female rape victims' perpetrators: acquaintance 56.1%, intimate partner 39.3%, family 16.0%, **stranger 12.1%**.
*Implication for Matra:* the authoritative source for "most perpetrators are known" and for race and ethnicity prevalence. Use these numbers, not advocacy rounding.

**`hakimi_et_al_2018_negative_reactions_black_white_survivors.md`**
Hakimi, D., Bryant-Davis, T., Ullman, S. E., & Gobin, R. L. (2018). Relationship between negative social reactions to sexual assault disclosure and mental health outcomes of Black and White female survivors. *Psychological Trauma, 10*(3), 270–275. https://doi.org/10.1037/tra0000245 (PMC5468506)
Community sample of 622 survivors. Negative social reactions predicted PTSD, depression and problem drinking across races. Black women were more harmed by *low-to-moderate* negative reactions, and high negative reactions harmed both groups similarly. The review notes Black survivors receive more negative reactions, especially from police and medical staff, partly because of racialized stereotypes.
*Implication for Matra:* culturally responsive attorney matching and validating language matter even more for Black survivors. Track equity of outcomes.

**`pcar_nsvrc_poverty_and_sexual_violence_2007.pdf`**
Pennsylvania Coalition Against Rape / NSVRC (Dawgert, S.) (2007). *Poverty and Sexual Violence: Building Prevention and Intervention Responses — A Guide for Counselors and Advocates.* https://pcar.org/sites/default/files/pages-pdf/poverty_and_sexual_violence.pdf (NSVRC listing: https://nsvrc.org/publications/poverty-and-sexual-violence-building-prevention-and-intervention-responses-guide-counse)
Describes poverty and sexual violence as reciprocal. Poverty raises vulnerability (unsafe housing, transit, economic dependence on abusers), and sexual violence causes job loss, health costs and homelessness. In PA, rape crisis centers estimated **70–90%** of survivors served were on public assistance or struggling with basic needs. One study found 92% of homeless mothers had experienced severe physical or sexual abuse. Data are dated (2007).
*Implication for Matra:* must work free, on low-end phones and shared devices, with no costs that fall on the survivor. Attorney matching should foreground contingency, legal-aid and VCCO-funded options.

### 6. Prevention / culture

**`cdc_stop_sv_technical_package_2016.pdf`**
Basile, K. C., DeGue, S., Jones, K., Freire, K., Dills, J., Smith, S. G., & Raiford, J. L. (2016). *STOP SV: A Technical Package to Prevent Sexual Violence.* CDC. https://www.cdc.gov/violenceprevention/pdf/sv-prevention-technical-package.pdf (fetched via Internet Archive; CDC blocked automated download)
Five strategies: **S**ocial norms that protect, **T**eaching skills, **O**pportunities to empower girls and women, **P**rotective environments, and **S**upporting survivors to lessen harms. Evidence highlights: Safe Dates (56–92% less dating-violence victimization and perpetration vs controls, sustained 4 years); Green Dot (11% lower SV perpetration on the intervention campus); bystander programs; Coaching Boys into Men; Strong African American Families (parenting). The cost of rape in one state study (2009) was estimated at $4.7B.
*Implication for Matra:* Matra sits in the "Support Survivors to Lessen Harms" strategy. Use STOP SV language in funder pitches to place the product inside CDC's framework.

### 7. Pornography and sexual aggression
See `paywalled_citations_and_pornography_evidence.md`, section A. That covers Wright, Tokunaga & Kraus 2016 (JComm meta-analysis, 22 studies: significant association, stronger for verbal aggression, violent content as a likely amplifier) and Ferguson & Hartley 2022 (TVA meta-analysis: no association for nonviolent porn, weak for violent porn, weak longitudinal evidence, effects shrink in better-designed studies). Fight the New Drug is an advocacy nonprofit, so cite primary research instead.
*Implication for Matra:* keep this out of product claims. If raised in outreach, describe it as "contested; strongest signal for violent content" to keep credibility with academic and legal partners.

### 8. Rape myth acceptance
Citations only (paywalled): Payne, Lonsway & Fitzgerald 1999 (IRMA scale, 7 myth subtypes); Lonsway & Fitzgerald 1994; Edwards et al. 2011 (*Sex Roles*). See section D of the citations file.
*Implication for Matra:* intake prompts and attorney materials should avoid wording that echoes myths, such as "were you drinking?" or "did you say no clearly?" as framing questions.

### 9. Trauma-informed design for technology

**`chen_et_al_2022_trauma_informed_computing_chi.pdf`**
Chen, J. X., McDonald, A., Zou, Y., Tseng, E., Roundy, K., Tamersoy, A., Schaub, F., Ristenpart, T., & Dell, N. (2022). Trauma-informed computing: Towards safer technology experiences for all. *CHI '22*. https://doi.org/10.1145/3491102.3517475 (NSF public-access copy: https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10347453)
Adapts SAMHSA's trauma-informed principles into **six principles for computing: safety, trust, peer support, collaboration, enablement, and intersectionality**. Gives concrete examples across UX research and design, security and privacy, AI/ML, and tech-company culture. Draws on work with IPV survivors and transgender users.
*Implication for Matra:* use the six principles as a design-review checklist, e.g. predictable flows, no surprises, user control over pace and deletion, and intersectional testing.

**`nnedv_safetynet_retention.md`**, **`nnedv_safetynet_encryption_basics.md`**, **`nnedv_safetynet_data_security_checklist.pdf`**
NNEDV Safety Net Project. *FAQs on Record Retention and Deletion*; *Encryption Basics for Programs*; *Data Security Checklist.* https://www.techsafety.org/retention, https://www.techsafety.org/encryption-basics-for-programs, https://www.techsafety.org/s/NNEDV_DataSecurity_English08_access-amdl.pdf (Agency toolkit index: https://www.techsafety.org/resources-agencyuse)
Key principles: "a survivor's data belongs to the survivor"; collect the **minimum** needed; collect ranges rather than identifiers (an age range, not a date of birth); purge on a schedule; restrict access by role; data practices must never increase risk to victims. The NNEDV App Safety Center (reviewed 40+ survivor apps; https://techsafety.org/blog/2015/8/5/new-app-safety-center-helps-victims-and-their-advocates-navigate-smartphone-apps) has a developer-considerations section. The page could not be fetched automatically, so review it manually.
*Implication for Matra:* directly validates zero-knowledge, data-minimization and survivor-controlled deletion. Cite NNEDV in privacy docs and funder applications.
