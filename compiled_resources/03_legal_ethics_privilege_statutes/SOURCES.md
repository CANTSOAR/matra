# 03 — Legal ethics, privilege, victims' rights, and compensation statutes

Compiled 2026-10-06 for the NJ survivor-intake platform (consent-based sharing with verified attorneys).

> Not legal advice. These are research copies; check the official current text, or ask NJ counsel, before relying on any provision.

All downloads were checked with `file`. HTML pages were converted to `.md`/`.txt` with Python (BeautifulSoup, PyMuPDF). Nothing behind a paywall was downloaded.

## 1. Federal Crime Victims' Rights Act (CVRA)

**File:** `18USC3771_CVRA.md`
**Citation:** 18 U.S.C. § 3771
**URL:** https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/3771 (uscode.house.gov was down for maintenance when fetched)
**Why it matters:** This lists the federal victims' rights, including the § 3771(a)(8) right "to be treated with fairness and with respect for the victim's dignity and privacy" and the right to confer with the prosecutor. It covers **federal** prosecutions only; NJ state cases use Art. I ¶22 and N.J.S.A. 52:4B-34 et seq. (item 4). It is also the basis for the victim-notice rule in Fed. R. Crim. P. 17(c)(3) (item 6).

## 2. Uniform Victims of Crime Act (1992)

**File:** `Uniform_Victims_of_Crime_Act_1992.md` (full act with prefatory note and comments)
**Citation:** Uniform Victims of Crime Act (Nat'l Conf. of Comm'rs on Uniform State Laws 1992; approved by the ABA Feb. 9, 1993)
**URL:** https://web.archive.org/web/2005/http://www.law.upenn.edu/bll/archives/ulc/fnact99/1990s/uvca92.htm. This is the archived ULC final-acts archive at U. Penn. I could not find a current copy on uniformlaws.org.
**Why it matters:** It is a full model of victim participation rights plus a compensation scheme (an agency, awards, restitution). That makes it a useful checklist of rights an intake product could track.
**How few states adopted it:** I found **no state that enacted it as a uniform act.** The ULC later reclassified it as a **model act** (also called the "Model Victims of Crime Act"), and that only happens when a uniform act fails to gain adoptions. Minnesota bills in 1995 (HF 1955 / SF 1764) proposed adopting its reparations article, but I found no sign they passed. Its predecessor, the Uniform Crime Victims Reparations Act (1973), was enacted in only 5 states, according to the UVCA's own prefatory note. Treat "zero enactments" as **very likely but not confirmed**: the ULC no longer publishes an enactment map for this act.

**Why it matters:** VCCO is the most direct funding source for attorneys who help survivors. N.J.S.A. 52:4B-8 and N.J.A.C. 13:75-4.10 pay a lawyer either for handling the VCCO claim itself or for up to $10,000 of "legal assistance" in matters arising from the victimization. Under 52:4B-2, "legal assistance" includes enforcing victims' rights, restraining orders, family law, employment, and public benefits. The 9-month police-report requirement (52:4B-18; N.J.A.C. 13:75-3.1(b)) is a hard eligibility gate, so intake should capture whether and when the crime was reported to police.

Caution: the separate HTML statute page at nj.gov/oag/njvictims/stat/52_4b-18.htm is **out of date** (it still says "three years"). Use the 2020 compilation or the regulations.

## 4. NJ victims' rights

| File | Citation / source |
|---|---|
| `NJ_Constitution_Art_I_incl_para22.md` | N.J. Const. art. I, ¶22 (added eff. Dec. 5, 1991), full Article I. https://www.njleg.gov/constitution |
| `NJSA_52-4B-34_to_38_Crime_Victims_Bill_of_Rights.md` | N.J.S.A. 52:4B-34 to -38 as posted by NJ OAG (https://www.nj.gov/oag/njvictims/stat/52_4b-34.htm through -38.htm), plus an appendix with bill S1062 (2024 session), which reproduces the **current** 52:4B-36(a)–(r) as its base text. https://pub.njleg.gov/Bills/2024/S1500/1062_I1.HTM |

**Why it matters:** ¶22 makes victims' rights constitutional in NJ ("fairness, compassion and respect"; the right to be present at proceedings). 52:4B-36(r), added by P.L.2012, c.27, gives victims **standing to appear, including through a private attorney**, to enforce those rights. That is a concrete kind of matter attorneys can take, and VCCO may pay for it under 52:4B-8(b) for an otherwise eligible victim (VCCO-paid work is not pro bono).

Caveat: the OAG HTML pages leave out subsections (o)–(r). S1062 is a **pending bill**: in its official HTML, bracketed text is proposed deletion and underlined text is proposed addition, and the underlining is lost in the conversion. Confirm current text on the official N.J.S.A.

## 5. Corporate attorney-client privilege

**Files:** `Upjohn_v_US_449US383_1981.md` (LII text) and `Upjohn_v_US_449US383_1981_USReports.pdf` (official U.S. Reports PDF, Library of Congress)
**Citation:** Upjohn Co. v. United States, 449 U.S. 383 (1981)
**URLs:** https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/449/383 ; https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep449/usrep449383/usrep449383.pdf
**Why it matters:** Upjohn rejected the "control group" test. Privilege covers communications made at counsel's direction to obtain legal advice, but it protects only **communications, not underlying facts**. It is the basis for the "Upjohn warning," and it frames whether intake data gathered by a non-lawyer platform before any attorney is involved can be privileged (generally not, unless the platform is acting as the attorney's agent).

**Why it matters:**
- **Third-party doctrine (Miller, Smith).** Under Miller and Smith, information a user voluntarily gives a third party (a bank, a phone company, and by extension a **tech platform**) has no Fourth Amendment protection. The government can get it with a subpoena or a § 2703(d) order rather than a warrant.
- **Carpenter's limit.** Carpenter carved out historical cell-site location data (a warrant is required) but called itself a "narrow" decision. Survivor narratives stored on a platform are therefore presumptively reachable by government process. Data minimization and end-to-end/zero-knowledge design are what actually protect them.
- **Defense subpoenas (federal).** Rule 17(c)(3), added in 2008 to implement the CVRA, requires a **court order and notice to the victim** before the defense can subpoena "personal or confidential information about a victim" from a third party. That is a federal-court protection only.
- **NJ victim counselor privilege.** N.J.S.A. 2A:84A-22.13 to -22.15 makes communications with a "victim counselor" privileged. A victim counselor is someone at a victim counseling center with 40+ hours of training, working under a supervisor. The privilege also makes the counselor's records immune from discovery. *J.G.* read it as close to absolute: no in-camera review "in the absence of compelling circumstances," and waiver only by the victim's prior written consent.
- **The platform is not covered.** A **tech platform is not a "victim counseling center,"** so communications with it fall outside this privilege. A third party who is not "necessary to facilitate communication or further the counseling process" can also defeat confidentiality.
- **Massachusetts comparison.** The Massachusetts Bishop/Fuller/Dwyer line shows the contrasting approach: a structured judicial protocol for defense access to privileged counseling records.

## 7. Lawyer confidentiality and technology

| File | Citation | URL |
|---|---|---|
| `ABA_Model_Rule_1.6_Confidentiality.md` | ABA Model Rule of Prof'l Conduct 1.6 (rule and Comments [1]–[20]) | https://www.americanbar.org/groups/professional_responsibility/publications/model_rules_of_professional_conduct/rule_1_6_confidentiality_of_information/ (via Internet Archive 2024 snapshot; americanbar.org blocks scripted downloads) |
| `ABA_Formal_Opinion_477R_2017.pdf` | ABA Formal Op. 477R, "Securing Communication of Protected Client Information" (May 11, 2017; revised May 22, 2017) | https://www.americanbar.org/content/dam/aba/administrative/professional_responsibility/aba_formal_opinion_477.authcheckdam.pdf (via Internet Archive) |

**Why it matters:**
- **Rule 1.6(c)** requires "reasonable efforts" to prevent unauthorized access to client information. Comments [18]–[19] list the factors for judging what is reasonable: sensitivity, cost, the effect on the client, and so on.
- **Opinion 477R** applies that standard to electronic communications. It says highly sensitive matters may call for enhanced security such as encryption, and lawyers must vet vendors. Any attorney receiving survivor data through the platform will hold the platform to this standard.
- **NJ's own rule.** NJ attorneys are actually bound by NJ RPC 1.6, which is broadly similar but has NJ-specific mandatory-disclosure provisions. I did not download it; the official text is on njcourts.gov.

## README claim check

The README says: *"Learn the exact conditions under which the state reimburses attorney fees ($275/hr up to statutory caps), report filing deadlines (typically within 90 days), and claim filing limits."*

| README claim | Verdict | Correct information | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| "State reimburses attorney fees ($275/hr up to statutory caps)" | **Mostly correct, but incomplete** | **$275/hr is right** (N.J.A.C. 13:75-4.10(c); the statute says "$275 or more to be fixed by the office"). There are **two separate fee tracks**. **(a) Representing the claimant on the VCCO claim or appeal:** reasonable fees capped at **15% of the award**, paid **on top of** the award; minimum **$500** (also payable on a denial if the lawyer acted diligently); the attorney **may not charge the client more** (52:4B-8(a)(3)). The VCCO application describes this as "$275/hr or 15% of the total award, whichever is less." **(b) "Legal assistance" in other matters arising from the victimization** (victims'-rights enforcement, restraining orders, family law, employment, benefits): up to **$10,000** at $275/hr, counted within the **$25,000** overall award cap under 52:4B-18. The victim must otherwise be VCCO-eligible. | N.J.S.A. 52:4B-8, 52:4B-2 ("legal assistance"), 52:4B-18; N.J.A.C. 13:75-4.10; VCCO Application Instructions p. 4 |
| "Report filing deadlines (typically within 90 days)" | **Incorrect** | The crime must be **reported to police within 9 months** of the crime or its reasonable discovery, with a good-cause exception (N.J.S.A. 52:4B-18; N.J.A.C. 13:75-3.1(b)). The "90 days" is most likely the **VCCO's own deadline to decide a claim** (90 days after a complete application, 52:4B-18 / N.J.A.C. 13:75-4.2(c)), or the pre-2011 rule (3 months). It is **not** a deadline for the victim. | 2020 OAG statute compilation; N.J.A.C. 13:75-3.1; VCCO eligibility page; VCCO FAQ 2025 |
| "Claim filing limits" (unspecified); founder believes NJ extended filing to 5 years | **Confirmed: 5 years** | Application must be filed **within 5 years** of the injury or death (P.L.2019, c.380, eff. Aug. 1, 2020; the earlier limits were 2 years, then 3 years under P.L.2011, c.165). Late filing is allowed for **good cause**, which includes not being informed of VCCO benefits and crime-induced incapacity. For **minors**, the 5 years run from the 18th birthday. For **child sexual abuse**, they run from reasonable discovery (N.J.A.C. 13:75-3.1(a)(3)). | N.J.S.A. 52:4B-18 (as amended 2019, c.380); N.J.A.C. 13:75-3.1(a); VCCO eligibility page ("Changes as of August 1, 2020") |

A claim **expires 10 years** after the initial determination order, with exceptions. Payments come only after insurance and other sources are exhausted. **Suggested README replacement text:**
> NJ VCCO (N.J.S.A. 52:4B-1 et seq.; N.J.A.C. 13:75): the crime must be reported to police within 9 months, and the claim filed within 5 years (good-cause exceptions; minors from age 18). VCCO pays attorneys $275/hr: (a) for VCCO claim representation, up to 15% of the award (min. $500), paid on top of the award and with no fee charged to the client; (b) for related "legal assistance" (restraining orders, family law, victims'-rights enforcement, and so on), up to $10,000 within the $25,000 claim cap.
